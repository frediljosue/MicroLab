How real is this simulation?

      
Every board and part carries one of three labels, so you always know what you are trusting:

      
    
        
  • Simulated Logic-level simulation. Your Arduino code really runs, translated to JavaScript, and the libraries behave like the real ones: return values, buffers, addresses, timing rules. Voltages, currents and pin limits follow the datasheet. What is simplified: exact timing, analog noise, heat, and anything below the logic level, such as rise times or ESD.
    • 
        
  • Reference only Data, not execution. Specs, pinouts, wiring and common mistakes are given, but you can't place it and run code on it. Example: the micro:bit and Particle Photon.
    • 
        
  • Not executable Some things can't run here at all: Wi-Fi and Bluetooth traffic, real USB, interrupts at true hardware speed, PIO, deep sleep and bootloader behavior. The lab says so instead of faking it.
    • 
      

      
Arduino C++ and MicroPython

      
    
        
  • Arduino C++ is compiled with real C/C++ rules for the selected board: int is 16-bit on AVR boards and 32-bit elsewhere, integer division truncates, overflow wraps (with a warning), and char arrays, structs, classes, references and sprintf behave like the real thing. Templates, inheritance and dynamic memory are not supported.
    • 
        
  • MicroPython (Pico and ESP boards) runs a subset of the language with MicroPython's rules: arbitrary-size ints, single-precision floats, / vs //, and real tracebacks. Modules: machine (Pin, ADC, PWM, I2C, Timer, time_pulse_us), time, math, random, dht, neopixel, ssd1306. Not supported: generators, with, inheritance, the file system, networking and UART/SPI.
    • 
      

      
Values marked ≈ or "varies" depend on the module or clone you buy. Always check your own board's datasheet before high-current or mains work.

      
Wiring

      
    
        
  • Click a pin, then another pin, to connect them. Dragging from pin to pin works too. Esc cancels.
    • 
        
  • Hover over any pin to see its name, and its live voltage while running.
    • 
        
  • Breadboard: drop LEDs, resistors, buttons and transistors onto the holes and they snap in. Each a–e and f–j column strip is connected, and the rails run the full length. Dragging the breadboard carries its parts along.
    • 
        
  • Select a wire and press Delete to remove it. Wire colors are automatic: GND black, 5V red, 3.3V orange.
    • 
        
  • Organizing wires: ⌗ Tidy wires routes every wire with right-angle runs around the parts, spacing parallel wires apart. Tick Keep tidy to re-route as you move things.
    • 
        
  • Shaping a wire by hand: select it, drag a + to add a bend, drag the white dots, and double-click a dot to remove it. Hand-shaped wires lock automatically (orange dots), so Tidy never moves them. Untick the lock or press Auto-route to hand a wire back.
    • 
        
  • Scroll to zoom, drag the mat to pan, and press ⤢ to fit everything on screen.
    • 
      

      
3D view

      
    
        
  • Switch with 2D bench / 3D view at the top-left of the bench. The 3D view mirrors your project live: LEDs glow, servos and motors turn, and displays show your output.
    • 
        
  • In 3D you can orbit, zoom, drag parts around, and click buttons, switches, keypads and sensors. Wire in 2D.
    • 
        
  • Drive mode turns a two-motor project into a robot car in an obstacle arena. The car drives with real differential steering, and the HC-SR04 measures the actual distance to walls and boxes.
    • 
      

      
Interacting while it runs

      
    
        
  • Buttons: hold the red cap. Slide switch: click it. PIR: click the dome to wave a hand. Joystick: drag the knob.
    • 
        
  • Sensors: select the part and move its sliders for light, temperature, humidity, or distance.
    • 
      

      
What the simulator catches

      
    
        
  • LEDs with no resistor burn out, and the lab shows how many mA each LED draws.
    • 
        
  • Short circuits, pins fighting each other, and 5V on a 3.3V board's GPIO.
    • 
        
  • Floating inputs, a forgotten pinMode, PWM on a pin that can't do it.
    • 
        
  • Motors, lamps, or servos powered straight from a GPIO pin.
    • 
        
  • Wrong I²C pins or addresses (LCD, OLED, BMP280), and DHT11 vs DHT22 mix-ups.
    • 
        
  • A DS18B20 without its 4.7 kΩ pull-up, a MAX7219 left in shutdown, stepper coils in the wrong order, a keypad wired differently from rowPins/colPins, and a transistor base with no resistor.
    •

Differences from real hardware

      
    
        
  • Integer division isn't truncated: 7 / 2 gives 3.5. Use floor() when it matters.
    • 
        
  • Timing is close but not microsecond-exact. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth aren't simulated.
